Oil-Based and Protein Soot

Smoke from burning petroleum products like plastics, rubber, and synthetic materials produces oil-based soot. This residue is greasy, dark, and acidic. Oil-based soot eats into paint and corrodes metal. It's harder to remove than simple charcoal soot because it bonds chemically to surfaces.

Protein soot forms when protein-containing materials burn. Meat, eggs, wool, leather, and other protein-based substances release protein residue into smoke. This soot is tan or brown, not black. It's sticky and smells particularly foul. Protein soot is harder to deodorize than charcoal soot because the odor compounds are chemically embedded.

Typical household vacuums circulate air through standard filters that don't capture soot particles. Wet soot is the most common type of residue found after fires in homes. Wet soot results from fires that burned at lower temperatures and with limited oxygen supply. This type of soot is dark brown or black in color and has a sticky, wet appearance. It adheres to surfaces firmly and is difficult to remove. Wet soot typically occurs in fires where materials smolder rather than burning brightly. Structure fires, where flames are contained within walls or materials, often produce wet soot. Kitchen fires and fires in enclosed spaces tend to generate wet soot. The challenge with wet soot is that it smears easily and can spread contamination when touched or disturbed. If you wipe wet soot with a dry cloth, you will simply spread it around rather than removing it.
